**11. Why should atropine be avoided in glaucoma?**
_[Atropine]_

**Book answer:** Because atropine increases intraocular pressure, so it is contraindicated in glaucoma and lens luxation. It also reduces tear production, so it should be avoided in keratoconjunctivitis sicca.

**AI answer:**

Avoid atropine in glaucoma because it increases intraocular pressure (IOP) by several mechanisms:

- Atropine causes cycloplegia that decreases aqueous outflow, and this effect can increase IOP. [1]  
- Cycloplegia from atropine can cause ciliary cleft collapse, which may induce ocular hypertension and ultimately worsen glaucoma. [2]  
- Atropine increases the likelihood of peripheral anterior synechia across the iridocorneal angle and thereby predisposes to glaucoma development. [1]

Practice implications:
- IOP should be monitored in eyes before and during atropine administration, and the drug should not be given in glaucomatous or preglaucomatous eyes. [1]  
- Atropine is contraindicated in patients with narrow‑angle glaucoma (and glaucoma in general) because it increases intraocular pressure. [3][4]

Limitations of the provided evidence:
- These claims do not quantify how large, how often, or how long IOP increases occur, nor do they provide specific thresholds, incidence rates, species differences, or alternative management strategies.

**15. Why is buprenorphine not recommended for premedication when potent opioids will be needed during surgery?**
_[Buprenorphine]_

**Book answer:** Because it is a partial agonist that antagonizes the effects of full opioid agonists such as morphine and fentanyl.

**AI answer:**

Main reasons buprenorphine is not recommended for premedication when potent opioids will be needed during surgery

- Buprenorphine is a partial mu opiate/OP3 agonist and, as a partial agonist, antagonizes the effects of full OP3 (mu) agonists (e.g. morphine, fentanyl). [1][2]  
- The BSAVA formulary explicitly states: “do not use for premedication when administration of potent opioids during surgery is anticipated.” [2]  
- Buprenorphine’s high affinity for mu receptors, slow dissociation and prolonged duration of action make it difficult to reverse with naloxone, which would complicate attempts to restore full-opioid effects intra‑/post‑operatively. [1][2][3]

**55. Why is ivermectin not recommended in collies?**
_[Ivermectin]_

**Book answer:** Collies and related breeds are sensitive, so MDR-1 gene testing should be considered before use in these breeds.

**AI answer:**

Ivermectin is contraindicated in collies (and in Shetland sheepdogs, Old English sheepdogs, Australian shepherds, other herding breeds, and crosses with these breeds). [1][2]

The reason is a deletion mutation of the multidrug-resistance gene (ABCB1) that produces a truncated, nonfunctional P‑glycoprotein; P‑glycoprotein normally pumps ivermectin from the brain back into the blood. _[Desmopressin]_

Recommended dose: 1 μg/kg SC; given 30 minutes before surgery for dogs with mild to moderate Type 1 vWD. [1]

Do not give desmopressin more than once daily because of risk of water retention, hyponatremia, and tachyphylaxis. [2]
